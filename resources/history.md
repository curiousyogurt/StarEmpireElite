## From The *Sumerian Game* to *Star Empire Elite*

*Star Empire Elite* is a multiplayer strategy game played in the browser, turn by turn, in the tradition of the space-empire games that once ran on dial-up bulletin boards. This essay is about where that tradition comes from, and I want to begin with a claim strong enough to disagree with: the resource-management empire game was essentially complete in 1964. *The Sumerian Game* was an educational simulation written for schoolchildren, and already contained the whole form. The player governed a polity. Time advanced in turns. Resources were scarce, decisions took effect on a delay, and chance intervened whenever it liked. Prudent choices sometimes failed, and some disasters could only be endured. Everything the genre has produced in the sixty years since — the spaceships, the tech trees, the sprawling multiplayer economies — is elaboration on that original structure rather than invention. In the whole history of the form there are only two wellspring discoveries, and *The Sumerian Game* made the first of them.

The first discovery is easiest to see in *Hamurabi*, the best-known direct descendant of *The Sumerian Game*. It stripped the original of nearly everything. Gone was the educational framing, the narrative, any pretence of presentation. What remained was a terminal printing numbers and terse explanations. You bought land, planted grain, fed your people, and waited for the next year's report. The game was addictive anyway. The appeal never depended on an elaborate presentation; rather, it depended on the logic of constrained rule: subsistence, growth, and risk balanced inside a system legible enough to reason about but never quite stable enough to master. That is the first discovery, and *Hamurabi* is its proof: managing scarce resources under pressure and uncertainty could be play.

The second discovery had to wait for bulletin board systems. It was not a mechanic; it was other people. When the empire game went multiplayer with *Space Empire Elite* (whose name we have deliberately chosen to echo), scarcity stopped being something you managed against a computer algorithm, and became something you managed among rivals. This shifted the meaning of everything already in the game. Resources became leverage. Growth became provocation. Defence became signalling. The social aspects did not have to be designed in; they emerged naturally, because rule became both shared and contested. So the second discovery is this: managing resources could be social; and once it was social, it became political.

The medium then added a refinement of its own, almost by accident. A BBS took one caller at a time, so these games ran in turns spread across hours and days. This limitation produced something no design document asked for: the march of time. The empire persisted while you were away. Other players acted in your absence. Plans ripened slowly, damage accumulated, grudges had time to harden. An empire stopped being a table of resources that were immediately responded to, and became a position in an ongoing world: something you held, precariously, among others holding theirs.

Everything afterward is variation on these two discoveries. *Space Dynasty* carried the form into the PC BBS scene; *Solar Realms Elite* built bigger and more intricate empires atop it; *TradeWars* tilted the same skeleton toward commerce. These are real achievements, and it is not my intention to diminish them. But they confirm my thesis rather than complicating it: these variations thrived because the structure was already sound.

The commercially published empire-builders of the 1990s and later are a different story, and worth pausing over, because what they added works against the discoveries rather than extending them. They added speed and spectacle. Speed, because many of them made time real: the world no longer waited for your decision, and deliberation itself became a cost. The player who paused to think was the player falling behind, and judgement gave way to reflexes and rehearsed routines executed quickly. Spectacle, because the presentation *Hamurabi* had shown to be unnecessary came back as the main attraction: the screen filled, and the numbers receded behind it. I do not say these games are bad; many are superb at what they do. But what they do is different. Speed and spectacle are pleasures of another kind, and they have a way of crowding out the old pleasure of considered judgement, which needs planning, patience, and time that belongs to the player rather than to the clock.

*Star Empire Elite* is built on a wager: that the two wellspring discoveries of the genre are load-bearing, and the spectacle that came later is dispensable. Its turn is still *Hamurabi*'s turn, run through a longer ledger: income, expenses, exchange, building, action, espionage, outcomes. Calculation first; consequences next. Its world is still the BBS world, minus the modem: the game goes on while you are away, and your rivals act even when you do not. Nothing about this is nostalgia, or not merely, anyway. It is closer to restoration: taking a form that was buried under its own elaborations, and asking whether the engine still runs.

I think it does, and the reason is the one the whole lineage points to. The deepest pleasure these games offer was never spectacle but rather the challenge of ruling well: a sequence of hard decisions made under pressure, with incomplete information, against consequences that cannot be fully contained; and made, best of all, in the company of others playing too. *The Sumerian Game* discovered that managing resources could be play. The bulletin boards discovered that managing resources could be social. *Star Empire Elite* is a bet that those two discoveries, taken together and without spectacle, are still enough. That is a bet you can test yourself; the empire is waiting.
